\chapter{Demystifying Spatiotemporal Scaling Laws of Multimodal Video Embeddings}
\label{ch:spatiotemporal-scaling}

\begin{chapterabstract}
As text-video retrieval becomes essential for large-scale video applications, models are expected to handle a diverse range of queries, from broad thematic summaries to descriptions of fleeting moments. However, since long videos are typically processed via sparse sampling, it remains unclear how sampling density affects retrieval across varying granularities. To address this, we introduce MG-LVR, a benchmark of multi-granular queries over a long-video corpus, constructed via a sparse-to-dense, semantics-aware pipeline. Evaluation across 23 multimodal embedding models reveals a significant granularity gap: fine-grained retrieval lags coarse-grained performance by over 75\% under sparse sampling. Our analysis of spatiotemporal scaling shows that while spatial resolution is most beneficial at low frame counts, the optimal token allocation shifts systematically with query granularity. We further observe a training-time asymmetry where temporal representations are heavily anchored to specific frame counts. Leveraging these insights, we propose a progressive dense-to-sparse self-distillation framework that improves fine-grained query performance by up to 20\%. This work provides a granularity-aware perspective on spatiotemporal scaling, offering principled designs for more robust video embedding training and inference.
\end{chapterabstract}

\section{Introduction}

Text-video retrieval has been studied primarily in settings where a query summarizes the entirety of a short clip~\cite{wang2025cross}. However, real-world videos are increasingly long and informationally dense, spanning tens of minutes to hours~\cite{henschel2025streamingt2v,weng2024longvlm}. Within such videos, a user query may describe anything from the overall theme to a brief, specific moment, making retrieval inherently multi-granular~\cite{xiao2022video,chen2023joint}. Since encoding every frame at full resolution is computationally prohibitive~\cite{hu2022mmsampler}, models must rely on spatiotemporal sampling to produce video embeddings, which is a process proven critical for retrieval quality~\cite{guo2025gve}. The sampling strategy thus determines the available visual evidence. Too few frames or too low a resolution can cause the target moment to be missed entirely, constituting a structural information loss. Therefore, how sampling density interacts with query granularity is a central question for long-video retrieval. Nevertheless, while previous works have demonstrated the sensitivity of performance to sampling~\cite{guo2025gve,zhu2025dynamic}, this relationship remains underexplored because no existing benchmark provides the multi-granular and controllable testbed required to isolate these effects.

Current benchmarks are insufficient for this purpose. Short-clip datasets such as MSR-VTT~\cite{xu2016msr} and DiDeMo~\cite{anne2017localizing} are too brief for sampling density to have a meaningful impact. Recent long-video retrieval benchmarks~\cite{cai2025lovr} extend the temporal scope but evaluate at a single query granularity, making it impossible to determine whether sampling configurations should vary with query specificity. No existing setting jointly provides long-form videos and multi-granular query categorization within a unified evaluation framework. Such a combination is essential for exploring spatiotemporal scalability across diverse semantic scales.

To address this gap, we introduce the Multi-Granular Long Video Retrieval (\benchmarkname) benchmark shown in Figure~\ref{mglvr:fig:statistics}. \benchmarkname pairs long-form videos with queries categorized by temporal granularity, defined as the fraction of the video duration that a query covers. Importantly, the benchmark allows the relevant evidence for a query to be grounded either in a single clip or across multiple clips that may occur at different timestamps, reflecting realistic retrieval scenarios. Evaluation across 23 models reveals a pronounced granularity gap: fine-grained retrieval lags coarse-grained performance by over 75\% under sparse sampling and recovers only marginally as frame count increases. This provides a clear signal for studying how sampling configurations affect retrieval performance across different semantic granularities.

Using \benchmarkname, we conduct a comprehensive spatiotemporal scaling analysis across both Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining (CLIP)-based~\cite{gorti2022x} and Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM)-based families~\cite{kong2025modality}. At test time, increasing spatial resolution helps most when frames are few, while its benefit saturates at higher frame counts. Under a fixed token budget, the optimal spatial-temporal allocation shifts with query granularity. Coarse queries favor fewer frames at higher resolution, while fine-grained queries favor more frames at lower resolution. At training time, the interaction between spatial and temporal density is asymmetric. Spatial representations are resolution-robust, maintaining accuracy across varying input resolutions. Temporal representations exhibit frame-count anchoring, where performance peaks when the inference frame count matches the training configuration and degrades significantly when they diverge, particularly for fine-grained queries.

This analysis highlights a key insight that the knowledge required for effective low-density inference is already latent within high-density models, but it does not automatically transfer when the sampling configuration changes. To address this, we propose Video Embedding Waterfall (VEterfall), a progressive dense-to-sparse self-distillation framework. Inspired by the waterfall effect, our method cascades high-fidelity semantic knowledge from densely sampled embeddings down to sparser representations. In this way, VEterfall achieves up to a 20\% relative recall gain on fine-grained queries while operating in a 4$\times$ to 16$\times$ more efficient inference setting. Our experiments confirm that the scaling behaviors identified by \benchmarkname can be directly and effectively exploited in practice.

In summary, our contributions are: (1) \benchmarkname, the first benchmark for multi-granular long-video retrieval that enables scalable research into spatiotemporal sampling; (2) a multi-dimensional scaling analysis identifying key principles such as frame-count anchoring and granularity-dependent token allocation; and (3) VEterfall, a self-distillation method that enhances video embeddings under sparse sampling by unlocking multi-granular knowledge already latent within the models.

\section{Related Works}

\subsection{Video Retrieval Benchmarks}

Early text-video retrieval benchmarks such as MSR-VTT~\cite{xu2016msr} and DiDeMo~\cite{anne2017localizing} consist of short clips in which sparse frame sampling suffices to capture the full content, rendering sampling strategy largely inconsequential. Subsequent benchmarks have extended the scope in complementary directions: LoVR~\cite{cai2025lovr} targets retrieval over hour-long videos, while TVR~\cite{lei2020tvr} and ActivityNet Captions~\cite{krishna2017dense} provide fine-grained temporal annotations for partially relevant video retrieval~\cite{dong2022partially}. Despite this progress, each benchmark addresses only one dimension of the problem, covering either video duration or query granularity, but not both. \benchmarkname addresses this gap by pairing a long-form corpus with temporally categorized queries, enabling the first controlled study of how spatiotemporal sampling interacts with retrieval granularity.

\subsection{Video Embedding Models}

Video-language embedding models have evolved from frame-level temporal aggregation~\cite{luo2022clip4clip} to dedicated spatiotemporal encoders, dividing into two families. CLIP-based models extend contrastive image-text pretraining to video, with representative examples including InternVideo2~\cite{wang2024internvideo2} and X-CLIP~\cite{gorti2022x}. MLLM-based models repurpose large multimodal language models for embedding extraction, as exemplified by GVE~\cite{guo2025gve}, Qwen3-VL-Embedding~\cite{qwen3vlembedding}, and other modality-unified approaches~\cite{kong2025modality}. Scaling-law studies in this domain have primarily examined model size and training data volume; a recent complementary effort~\cite{zhu2025dynamic} establishes power-law scaling with respect to frame count and caption density on short clips. The present work differs by evaluating end-to-end multimodal embedding models on long-form video, decomposing sampling into orthogonal spatial and temporal axes, and introducing query granularity as a first-class variable. Building on the resulting scaling principles, we further propose VEterfall, a progressive dense-to-sparse self-distillation framework distinguished from conventional knowledge distillation by its cascaded, density-descending regime.




\section{Rethinking Text-Video Retrieval Evaluation}
\label{mglvr:sec:benchmark}

Text-video retrieval has historically been formulated as a global-matching task, where each query describes the entirety of its paired video. This convention reflects the structural limitations of existing benchmarks: queries are either brief summaries of short clips or document-level aggregations of dense captions, making the query and video co-extensive by construction and effectively sidestepping the challenge of temporal scale. In the era of long, multi-scene video, this paradigm is insufficient. User intent may range from identifying a moment of a few seconds within an hour-long lecture to capturing the overarching theme of an entire documentary, imposing qualitatively different demands on spatiotemporal sampling and representation density.

As shown in Figure~\ref{mglvr:fig:statistics}, the \textbf{Multi-Granular Long Video Retrieval (MG-LVR)} benchmark is designed to bridge this gap by formalizing \textit{temporal granularity} as an explicit evaluation axis. Unlike traditional datasets~\cite{lei2020tvr,krishna2017dense} where granularity emerges as a byproduct of annotator bias and video readability~(e.g., length, dynamics, complexity), \benchmarkname employs an automated pipeline that explicitly controls query granularity by analyzing semantic relationships among clips within each video, enabling a systematic study of how models navigate the spectrum from localized events to global narratives. The resulting benchmark contains $9,971$ queries paired with a corpus of $4,844$ videos averaging $705.9$ seconds in duration. As summarized in Table~\ref{mglvr:tab:benchmark_comparison}, \benchmarkname is the only benchmark that jointly covers all three granularity levels while supporting both single-clip and multi-clip evidence structures. The dataset and code of MG-LVR will be publicly released upon acceptance.

\subsection{Formulations}
\label{mglvr:sec:formulation}

\begin{definition}[\textbf{Text-Video Retrieval}]
    Given a text query $q$ and a video corpus $\mathcal{V}$, the task is to identify the target video $v^{*} \in \mathcal{V}$ whose content is described by $q$. The query may refer to any portion of the video, from a brief localized event to the overall theme, with the fraction of relevant content serving as a variable difficulty factor.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[\textbf{Temporal Granularity}]
Temporal granularity $G$ is defined as the ratio of relevant content duration to total video length:
\begin{equation}
    G = \frac{|\mathcal{S}(v^{*},\,q)|}{d_{\mathrm{video}}} \in (0,\,1].
    \label{mglvr:eq:granularity}
\end{equation}
As $G \to 0$, retrieval approaches a needle-in-a-video problem in which the target moment occupies a vanishingly small fraction of the video. For systematic evaluation, we partition the granularity spectrum into three categories: \textbf{Fine-grained (FN)}, \textbf{Medium-grained (MD)}, and \textbf{Coarse-grained (CR)}.
\end{definition}

In this work, granular thresholds are set to $G \in [0.001, 0.01]$ for FN and $G \in [0.1, 0.3]$ for MD, ensuring high inter-class distinctiveness and enabling clear observation of how model performance scales with task difficulty.

For the temporal distribution of relevant content, we further define temporal continuity $C$ to characterize the structural complexity of each retrieval target:
\begin{equation}
    C = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(e_i - s_i)}{e_k - s_1} \in (0,\,1],
    \label{mglvr:eq:continuity}
\end{equation}
where $[s_i, e_i]$ are the $k$ disjoint intervals composing $\mathcal{S}$. A value of $C=1$ indicates a single contiguous event, while $C<1$ represents fragmented content distributed across multiple disjoint segments, such as a recurring theme.

\subsection{MG-LVR: Sparse-to-Dense Benchmark Construction}
\label{mglvr:sec:construction}

We construct \benchmarkname through a semantics-aware pipeline that formalizes video retrieval as a sampling task over structured semantic relationships among clips. The core insight is to represent each video as a similarity graph, enabling the identification of retrieval units that vary in temporal granularity and evidence composition. This approach ensures that \benchmarkname covers scenarios where relevant evidence is grounded either in a single clip or across multiple clips that may occur at different timestamps.

\paragraph{Step 1: Material Curation.}
We build upon the Vript dataset~\cite{yang2024vript}, selecting long videos ($>120$s) with dense, high-quality GPT-4V clip-level captions. These captions serve as semantic proxies, allowing the pipeline to model video content without the prohibitive cost of pixel-level processing during construction.

\paragraph{Step 2: Semantic Topology Modeling.}
To provide a structural basis for unit extraction, each video is represented as a \textit{Clip Relation Graph}, where nodes correspond to clips, and edges correspond to the cosine similarity values between two clip caption embeddings produced by Qwen3-Embedding-8B~\cite{qwen3embedding}. This graph captures semantic relationships among clips: clusters of highly connected nodes often correspond to dominant themes, while weaker connections may indicate localized or transient events.

\paragraph{Step 3: Discovery of Informative Units.}
Retrieval units are extracted from the clip relation graph by applying structural constraints that correspond to our granularity and evidence composition settings. This graph-based discovery allows the benchmark to include both single-clip evidence and semantically related clips that appear at different timestamps.
\textit{Single-Clip Units:} Individual nodes with nearly zero external connections are selected as standalone retrieval units, yielding $32,984$ units.
\textit{Multi-Clip Units:} Identified via Louvain community detection to capture thematically consistent clips that may be dispersed across the timeline, yielding $7,764$ units.

\paragraph{Step 4: Retrieval Query Synthesis.}
An LLM (Qwen3-32B~\cite{yang2025qwen3}) synthesizes the metadata of each unit into a natural language query. For multi-clip units, the model is prompted to generate a holistic description covering the relationships among multiple relevant clips. A subsequent refinement pass filters out ambiguous descriptions and removes references to audio content to ensure a focus on visual information.

\paragraph{Step 5: Retrieval Query Categorization.}
Each query-video pair is assigned to a granularity level (FN, MD, or CR) based on the computed value of $G$. The final benchmark retains $9,971$ queries in total, comprising $4,706$, $4,142$, and $1,123$ queries for FN, MD, and CR, respectively.

\paragraph{Human Validation.}
To ensure retrieval uniqueness, a cross-modal collision check is applied: queries exhibiting high semantic similarity to non-target clips within the same video are pruned. Human validation confirms a $96\%$ accuracy rate over $50$ query-video pairs, with failure cases detailed in the supplementary material.

\section{Spatiotemporal Scaling Laws: Experiments, Analysis, and Discoveries}
\label{mglvr:sec:scaling}

Leveraging \benchmarkname as a controlled testbed, we systematically analyze how spatiotemporal sampling density affects retrieval performance across query granularity levels. The analysis proceeds in three phases: (i) baseline comparison under default configurations; (ii) test-time scaling, in which spatial and temporal densities are varied during inference; and (iii) training-time scaling, in which the density used during model training is adjusted to examine its effect on the quality of the learned embeddings.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Model Selection.}
Twenty-three models from two paradigms are evaluated: 6 CLIP-based models and 17 MLLM-based models.

\paragraph{Evaluation Protocol.}
Retrieval performance is measured using Recall@1 (R@1) and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) across three query granularity levels: Fine-grained (FN), Medium-grained (MD), and Coarse-grained (CR). The total visual token budget per video is defined as $N_{\text{total}} = F \times T$, where $F$ denotes the number of uniformly sampled frames and $T$ denotes the number of spatial tokens per frame as determined by the visual encoder. In the analyses that follow, $F$ and $T$ are varied independently while all other experimental factors are held constant to isolate the effect of sampling density.

\subsection{Baseline Comparison}
\label{mglvr:sec:baseline}

All twenty-three models are evaluated under two configurations (8 and 128 frames; results at 32 frames are provided in the supplementary material). As shown in Table~\ref{mglvr:tab:main_results_8f} and Table~\ref{mglvr:tab:main_results_128f}, performance degrades consistently from CR to FN across all architectures, regardless of model family, parameter scale, or training data composition. For Qwen3-VL-Emb-8B, R@1 drops from $0.8557$ on CR to $0.2102$ on FN at 8 frames, a relative decline of over 75\%. Even at 128 frames, FN R@1 improves only modestly to $0.2871$, a gain of less than 8 percentage points despite a $16\times$ increase in temporal sampling density. This pronounced granularity gradient indicates that simply increasing the number of frames yields strongly diminishing returns on fine-grained retrieval.

\subsection{Test-Time Scaling}
\label{mglvr:sec:test_time}

We examine how varying spatiotemporal sampling density at inference time affects retrieval performance, isolating spatial and temporal effects by fixing one dimension while varying the other.

\paragraph{Spatial Scaling.}
For MLLM-based models, the benefit of increasing spatial tokens per frame depends critically on the available temporal context (Figure~\ref{mglvr:fig:spatial_scaling_2}). When the number of frames is low ($F \leq 8$), higher spatial resolution yields consistent improvements. As $F$ increases beyond 32, the marginal gain from additional spatial tokens diminishes and occasionally reverses, particularly for CR queries where holistic scene understanding is more important than fine spatial detail. This suggests that spatial and temporal information are complementary rather than independently additive.

\paragraph{Temporal Scaling.}
Varying $F$ from 4 to 512 frames reveals a divergence in scaling behavior across model families (Figure~\ref{mglvr:fig:temporal_scaling}). MLLM-based models exhibit continuous improvement up to $F = 128$, with the largest absolute gains on FN queries. CLIP-based models, by contrast, plateau around $F = 16$ and exhibit performance degradation on FN and MD queries at higher frame counts. This difference is consistent with the underlying architectural design: MLLM-based models process frames jointly through a transformer with positional encodings, whereas CLIP-based models aggregate frame-level features via mean pooling, which dilutes discriminative signals as $F$ increases.

\paragraph{Spatiotemporal Trade-off.}
When $N_{\text{total}}$ is held constant, the optimal allocation between $F$ and $T$ depends on query granularity (Figure~\ref{mglvr:fig:tradeoff}). CR queries favor high-resolution, low-frame-count configurations, while FN queries favor more frames at lower spatial resolution. Reducing spatial tokens primarily discards fine-grained texture while preserving semantic content, whereas reducing the number of frames risks eliminating visual evidence of brief events entirely.

\paragraph{Temporal Scaling: Frame-Count Anchoring.}
Unlike spatial representations, temporal representations are tightly coupled to the frame count seen during training, a phenomenon we term \textit{frame-count anchoring}. A mismatch between $F_{\text{train}}$ and $F_{\text{test}}$ consistently degrades performance in both directions, but the degradation is asymmetric. When $F_{\text{train}} \gg F_{\text{test}}$, the performance drop is more severe: a model trained at $F_{\text{train}} = 32$ and evaluated at $F_{\text{test}} = 4$ drops $0.0104$ in Fine R@1 relative to the matched $F_{\text{train}} = 4$ baseline (Group~1 in Table~\ref{mglvr:tab:train_temporal_scaling_internvid_50t}). In the inverse direction, a model trained at $F_{\text{train}} = 4$ and evaluated at $F_{\text{test}} = 32$ drops $0.0083$ in Fine R@1 relative to the matched $F_{\text{train}} = 32$ baseline (Group~4 in Table~\ref{mglvr:tab:train_temporal_scaling_internvid_50t}). Although these gaps are measured against different absolute performance levels and should be interpreted accordingly, the consistent pattern across groups suggests that low-frame-count training better preserves fine-grained discriminability under temporal mismatch than high-frame-count training does.



















\section{VEterfall: Channeling Scaling Potential for Self-Enhancement of Video Embedding Models}

Our analysis reveals a key asymmetry: spatial embeddings tolerate token compression well, whereas temporal ones are governed by frame-count anchoring, i.e., models evaluated with high frame-density inputs degrade when the frame count is reduced at inference time. This suggests that the knowledge required for low-density inference is already latent within representations produced under high-density input sampling, but is not automatically transferred when the sampling configuration changes. We therefore propose \textbf{Video Embedding Waterfall (VEterfall)}, a progressive dense-to-sparse self-distillation framework that explicitly propagates this knowledge along the sampling-density axis, from high- to low-density configurations stage by stage, analogous to water descending through successive levels of a waterfall as shown in Figure~\ref{mglvr:fig:veterfall}.

\subsection{Method}

\paragraph{Single Dense-to-Sparse Distillation Step.}
The basic unit of VEterfall is a single distillation step~\cite{hinton2015distilling} from a denser to a sparser input sampling configuration, where density refers jointly to the number of frames sampled from the video and the number of spatial tokens retained per frame at inference time. A frozen teacher processes inputs at higher sampling density, while a student—sharing the same architecture and initialized from the same pre-trained checkpoint $\mathcal{V}^{(0)}$ (here GVE-3B~\cite{guo2025gve})—is trained at a strictly lower density. The student is optimized to align its video embedding with that of the teacher via MSE loss, with an additional InfoNCE~\cite{oord2018representation} loss term ($\lambda{=}0.5$) to maintain video-text alignment by contrastive learning throughout density reduction.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{figures/mglvr/d2s.pdf}
    \caption{VEterfall progressively distills video embeddings across progressively sparser input sampling configurations using the pretrained model itself as the teacher at each stage.}
    \label{mglvr:fig:veterfall}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{$K$-Round Cascade.}
To support large overall density reductions while keeping each individual step within a reliable transfer range, VEterfall chains $K$ distillation steps into a cascade, where each round takes the previous checkpoint as its frozen teacher:
$\mathcal{V}^{(k)} \;\xleftarrow{\;\text{distill}\;}\; \mathcal{V}^{(k-1)},\ k = 1, \dots, K.$
Here $\mathcal{V}^{(0)}$ serves as the pre-trained video embedding model, and $K$ controls the granularity of the reduction schedule.

\paragraph{Instantiation.}
We set $K{=}2$. Starting from $\mathcal{V}^{(0)}$ at $(16\text{F},\,400\text{T})$, the first round produces $\mathcal{V}^{(1)}$ at $(8\text{F},\,200\text{T})$, and the second produces $\mathcal{V}^{(2)}$ at $(4\text{F},\,100\text{T})$. Across two rounds, the input token budget is reduced by $16\times$ with no modification to the inference architecture. Each round uses 100K text-video pairs from InternVid~\cite{wang2023internvid}, a substantially smaller data requirement than comparable models.

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced \benchmarkname, a benchmark for the controlled study of spatiotemporal sampling across query granularity levels in long-video retrieval. Evaluating $23$ models revealed a fundamental asymmetry between spatial and temporal scaling: spatial representations are resolution-robust, whereas temporal representations exhibit frame-count anchoring that is most pronounced on fine-grained queries. Under a fixed token budget, the optimal allocation between spatial and temporal tokens depends on query granularity. Building on these findings, we proposed VEterfall, a progressive dense-to-sparse self-distillation framework in which the high-density representations of a model are used to improve its own low-density embeddings without requiring external supervision, achieving up to ${\sim}20\%$ relative R@1 gains on fine-grained queries under sparse sampling. An important direction for future work is to develop architectures and training strategies that generalize robustly across spatiotemporal sampling configurations.
