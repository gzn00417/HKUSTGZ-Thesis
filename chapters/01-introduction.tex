\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Background and Motivation}\label{sec:intro-background}

Video retrieval is a central task in multimedia information systems, supporting content search, recommendation, personalization, and large-scale video discovery. Given a query, a retrieval system identifies relevant videos from a candidate collection and provides a compact set of candidates for subsequent ranking or decision-making. The rapid growth of online video content in scale, duration, diversity, and semantic complexity has made video retrieval an important research problem at the intersection of multimedia representation learning, information retrieval, and recommender systems.

Early research on video retrieval was commonly formulated as text-to-video matching over short clips. In this setting, each video is typically paired with one or more concise textual descriptions, and retrieval is evaluated by the alignment between the text query and the corresponding clip. This formulation has provided a productive foundation for video-language contrastive learning, spatiotemporal encoding, and multimodal embedding models. Contemporary video retrieval, however, extends beyond this controlled regime. Retrieval systems increasingly encounter heterogeneous query forms, richer semantic conditions, complex video content, and candidate collections whose size precludes exhaustive scoring. These developments shift video retrieval from a fixed benchmark matching problem to a broader methodological question: how can retrieval remain effective as the semantic scope, visual input, and search space of the problem continue to expand?

This thesis studies this question through three connected aspects of retrieval: semantic scale, observational scale, and search scale. Semantic scale defines the scope of relevance that a video retrieval representation is expected to model. Observational scale determines the visual evidence from which such a representation is formed. Search scale governs the extent to which the resulting retrieval signal can be applied over large candidate collections. Together, these aspects characterize a complete retrieval process: the representation defines the relevance space, visual observation determines the evidence available to the representation, and search determines whether the learned signal can be applied at corpus scale.

Semantic scale defines the breadth and structure of the relevance space in video retrieval. Real-world retrieval scenarios rarely reduce to a single form of query-video alignment. They may involve textual, visual, or composed queries; global scene-level semantics or fine-grained event details; temporal dynamics, spatial relations, and partial relevance; as well as both short clips and long-context videos. A general-purpose retrieval representation should therefore support diverse relevance conditions rather than optimize for one narrow matching pattern. The first study addresses this dimension by organizing heterogeneous retrieval capabilities, synthesizing targeted multi-task supervision, and training a general video embedder through curriculum learning. In this way, task organization and data synthesis expand the semantic scope of video retrieval representation learning.

Observational scale concerns the fidelity and selectivity with which visual evidence is preserved for retrieval. A video retrieval model cannot process a video at full spatiotemporal resolution, and should instead construct its representation from a finite set of sampled frames and visual tokens. This constraint creates a fundamental trade-off between temporal coverage and spatial detail under a fixed computational budget. Increasing the number of frames may improve the chance of capturing transient or localized events, whereas preserving more spatial tokens may better support queries that depend on object appearance, scene layout, or global visual semantics. The appropriate allocation is therefore not universal, but depends on the granularity and structure of the query. The second study investigates this dimension by analyzing retrieval behavior across temporal sampling densities, spatial token budgets, training densities, and inference densities, and further develops dense-to-sparse self-distillation to transfer retrieval capability from high-density observation to efficient sparse inference.

Search scale determines whether learned retrieval signals remain usable under the computational constraints of large candidate collections. Although small benchmarks often permit exhaustive similarity computation between a query and every candidate video, practical video retrieval and recommendation systems operate over collections that may contain hundreds of millions of items. At this scale, video representations are expensive to store, compare, and update, and exhaustive neural scoring is incompatible with online latency and throughput requirements. A useful retrieval signal should therefore be not only accurate, but also searchable and executable within large-scale serving systems. The third study addresses this dimension by co-designing learned retrieval metrics, graph-based approximate nearest neighbor search, and GPU-parallel execution. This design improves the compatibility between learned similarity functions and graph traversal, while accelerating irregular retrieval computation under high-concurrency large-corpus settings.

Taken together, these studies form a coherent research trajectory for scalable and generalizable video retrieval. The thesis begins with representation learning that expands the semantic and supervisory basis of video retrieval, proceeds to spatiotemporal sampling analysis and adaptation under varying visual density, and culminates in efficient candidate search over large video corpora. This trajectory reflects a unified view of video retrieval: effective retrieval depends on the relevance conditions represented by the model, the visual evidence preserved by sampling, and the efficiency with which the resulting retrieval signal is searched at scale. By addressing these aspects jointly, the thesis develops methods for video retrieval systems that support richer semantic tasks, adapt to different sampling budgets, and operate over large candidate spaces while maintaining effectiveness across heterogeneous queries, videos, and deployment conditions.

\section{Organization of the Thesis}\label{sec:intro-organization}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{ch:related-work} reviews the background and related literature on video retrieval, multimodal embeddings, multi-task retrieval, video sampling, long-video retrieval, approximate nearest neighbor search, graph retrieval, and large-scale candidate retrieval systems. Chapter~\ref{ch:universal-video-retrieval} presents the first study, which focuses on scalable video retrieval representation learning through capability-guided evaluation, targeted multi-task data synthesis, curriculum learning, and the General Video Embedder. Chapter~\ref{ch:spatiotemporal-scaling} extends the investigation to observational scale by analyzing retrieval behavior across temporal and spatial sampling densities and introducing dense-to-sparse self-distillation for efficient inference. Chapter~\ref{ch:scale-efficient-retrieval} advances the thesis toward search scale by studying search-friendly learned metrics, graph-based approximate nearest neighbor search, parallel graph traversal, and GPU execution under high-concurrency large-corpus settings. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes the thesis by summarizing the main contributions and discussing future directions, including adaptive sampling policies, retrieval-oriented video foundation models, joint optimization of embeddings and indexes, and evaluation protocols for real-world video retrieval. Finally, Chapter~\ref{ch:publications} lists the publications involved in this thesis.
