\chapter{Conclusion and Future Directions}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Modern video retrieval must operate across heterogeneous query forms, long and informationally dense videos, and candidate collections whose scale precludes exhaustive scoring. This thesis has argued that these demands are best understood as three sequential scaling challenges along the retrieval pipeline: semantic scaling, observational scaling, and search scaling. Each presents a distinct bottleneck that is not eliminated by optimizing an upstream stage alone.

The first study (Chapter~\ref{ch:universal-video-retrieval}) diagnosed capability gaps in fine-grained, temporal, and partially relevant retrieval across sixteen public datasets, then addressed them through a synthesis pipeline producing 1.55 million multi-task video-text pairs and a curriculum learning strategy using inherent task hierarchies. The resulting model achieved state-of-the-art zero-shot performance across diverse retrieval scenarios, and the evaluation reveals that partially relevant retrieval is a pivotal yet overlooked real-world challenge. The second study (Chapter~\ref{ch:spatiotemporal-scaling}) introduced the first multi-granular long-video retrieval benchmark and found, across twenty-three multimodal embedding models, that fine-grained retrieval lags coarse-grained performance by over 75\% under sparse sampling. This finding shows that observational scaling is a structural bottleneck rather than a secondary preprocessing choice. A dense-to-sparse inference framework built on this analysis achieved up to 20\% fine-grained improvement while operating 4$\times$ to 16$\times$ more efficiently. The third study (Chapter~\ref{ch:scale-efficient-retrieval}) proposed a GPU-accelerated graph retrieval framework with co-designed multi-relational metric learning, parallel graph search with adaptive GPU aggregation, and system-level optimizations including traffic dispersal, compilation optimization, and model quantization. Deployed across more than twenty applications at Baidu, the system serves hundreds of millions of users at hundreds of millions of requests per second, improving user time-on-page by more than 3\%. This study shows that learned retrieval signals must be co-designed with index structures and hardware execution, since retrieval accuracy alone is insufficient for deployment-scale search.

Across all three studies, a central finding is that scaling capacity alone, whether model size, observation density, or metric accuracy, does not automatically yield broader retrieval generalization unless aligned with the specific bottleneck at each pipeline stage. Generalizability serves as the empirical criterion for assessing whether learned capability transfers beyond the conditions under which it was optimized. In semantic scaling, transfer is assessed across query modalities, retrieval domains, and relevance granularities. In observational scaling, it is assessed across sampling densities, video durations, and query granularities. In search scaling, it is assessed across deployment contexts, traffic patterns, and serving constraints. These forms differ in their specifics but converge on the same principle: scaling is meaningful only when the resulting capability extends beyond the narrow condition under which it is optimized.

\section{Future Directions}
\label{sec:future-works}

Each study in this thesis also points toward capabilities that extend beyond the present work. This section outlines four directions for next-generation video retrieval systems, suggesting that video retrieval may evolve from a standalone embedding task into a foundational capability for multimodal intelligence.

\subsection{Retrieval-Native Multimodal Foundation Models}

Current multimodal large language models are typically trained for generation or instruction following, and retrieval is added afterward through contrastive fine-tuning or a separate retriever. This creates a tension: hidden states optimized for next-token prediction differ fundamentally from the compact, discriminative representations that retrieval requires. A promising direction is to design foundation models that are retrieval-native rather than retrieval-adapted, exposing retrieval-compatible hidden states through mechanisms such as retrieval-specific tokens, task-decoupled routing, or lightweight adapters, while preserving generative and instruction-following abilities. Such representations should also remain stable across spatiotemporal sampling configurations, addressing the frame-count anchoring observed in Chapter~\ref{ch:spatiotemporal-scaling}. The deeper goal is a retrieval-compatible interface inside the foundation model, so that dense embeddings inherit the model's world knowledge and multimodal reasoning. An open problem is how to achieve this without degrading generation quality.

\subsection{Video Retrieval as Long-Term Memory for Interactive and Embodied Agents}

Current benchmarks treat videos as fixed inputs, but deployed systems increasingly process continuous visual streams. Future retrieval systems may need to operate as streaming visual memory, continuously encoding incoming video and supporting real-time queries against a growing memory store. An agent in a dynamic environment may need to retrieve the last observed location of an object, recall relevant visual context from the past few hours, or detect anomalous events within a recent time window. These tasks require retrieval over temporally distributed evidence under strict latency constraints, connecting observational scaling to search scaling. Evaluation would shift beyond Recall@K toward downstream task success, decision latency, memory usefulness, and the ability to retrieve sparse events from continuous streams. In such systems, video retrieval becomes a memory access mechanism embedded in the perception-action loop of multimodal agents.

\subsection{Self-Evolving Data, Evaluation, and Curriculum Optimization}

The evaluation-guided synthesis strategy in Chapter~\ref{ch:universal-video-retrieval} shows that targeted data generation can close specific capability gaps more effectively than indiscriminate scaling. This principle can be extended into a self-evolving loop: an evaluator diagnoses weaknesses across fine-grained retrieval capabilities, a synthesizer generates targeted multimodal training examples, and a curriculum scheduler dynamically adjusts training distributions based on evaluation feedback. The updated model is then re-evaluated, closing the loop. Such a framework could reduce reliance on manually designed datasets and curricula, enabling retrieval models to continuously adapt to new query forms, video domains, and relevance patterns that static benchmarks cannot fully anticipate.

\subsection[Continuous Generative Representations for Multi-Granular Video Retrieval]{Continuous Generative Representations for\\Multi-Granular Video Retrieval}

Video semantics are inherently multi-granular, and a single pooled embedding may be insufficient for queries spanning different semantic resolutions. Diffusion and flow-based generative models transform representations through continuous trajectories, where different stages may expose representations at different levels of abstraction. Early denoising steps may capture global scene structure, while later steps may encode fine-grained action and temporal details. Future work could explore extracting retrieval embeddings from intermediate generative states, enabling a single model to produce multi-granular representations. This direction remains speculative: significant challenges remain in making generative trajectories discriminative for retrieval, and it is an open question whether such models can match purpose-built encoders at comparable cost.

Together, these directions suggest that video retrieval may evolve from a standalone embedding task into a foundational interface for multimodal intelligence, extending the thesis beyond scalable retrieval systems toward retrieval-centered multimodal systems that can remember, adapt, and reason over visual experience.
