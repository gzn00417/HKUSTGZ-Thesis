\section{Basic Concepts}
\label{sec:rw-concepts}

This section introduces the formal concepts and problem formulations underlying this thesis, organized along the core pipeline of video retrieval: problem formulation (\S\ref{sec:bc-vr}), embedding models (\S\ref{sec:bc-embedder}), spatiotemporal preprocessing (\S\ref{sec:bc-spatiotemporal}), and large-scale search (\S\ref{sec:bc-search}).

\subsection{Video Retrieval: Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:bc-vr}

Video retrieval is the task of identifying videos from a collection that satisfy a user's information need. Given a query $q$ and a video corpus $\mathcal{V} = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_N\}$ of $N$ candidate videos, the goal is to retrieve the subset of videos whose content is relevant to $q$~\citep{zhu2023deep}. In the ranking formulation, the system produces an ordered list of videos sorted by their estimated relevance scores $s(q, v_i)$.

Video retrieval systems support diverse query formats:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Textual Video Retrieval} (a.k.a., Text-to-Video Retrieval). The query $q$ is a natural language description, and the system retrieves videos matching the textual description. This is the most widely studied formulation.
    \item \textbf{Composed Video Retrieval}. The query $q$ combines multiple modalities, typically a text description and a reference image or video, supporting scenarios where users provide both visual examples and textual specifications to refine their search intent.
    \item \textbf{Visual Video Retrieval}. The query $q$ is a pure visual input (image or video), and the system retrieves semantically similar videos from the corpus.
\end{itemize}

Beyond query modality, video retrieval tasks vary in the granularity and nature of the semantic match required:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coarse-grained retrieval} matches videos at the level of overall themes, topics, or general content categories (e.g., ``a cooking tutorial''). The primary challenge lies in semantic abstraction: the system must capture the gist of a video rather than attend to specific visual details.
    \item \textbf{Fine-grained retrieval} requires precise understanding of detailed visual attributes within short clips, encompassing spatial relationships (\textit{spatial} retrieval, e.g., ``a person slicing onions with a ceramic knife on a wooden board''), temporal dynamics (\textit{temporal} retrieval, e.g., ``a person performing a roundhouse kick''), and partially relevant scenarios (\textit{partially relevant} retrieval), where only a short segment of a video matches the query while the remainder is irrelevant. The challenges are perceptual precision for spatial and temporal matching, and temporal localization under whole-video representations for partially relevant retrieval.
    \item \textbf{Long-context retrieval} involves queries that require holistic understanding of extended temporal context (such as narrative progression, causal chains, or cross-segment reasoning) rather than matching a single localized event. The challenge is temporal integration: the relevant evidence is distributed across the video and cannot be reduced to any individual segment.
\end{itemize}

Although these domains may appear similar in form, all mapping a query to a ranked list of videos, they pose fundamentally different challenges: coarse-grained retrieval tests semantic abstraction, fine-grained retrieval (spatial, temporal, and partially relevant) tests perceptual precision and temporal localization, and long-context retrieval tests temporal integration. This taxonomy of query modalities and semantic domains collectively defines the \textbf{semantic scale} of video retrieval: the breadth of relevance conditions that a retrieval representation must support. A separate challenge, \textit{long-video retrieval} on hour-long videos (e.g., movies, lectures, livestreams), is primarily an observational scaling problem concerning temporal sparsity at scale, which is examined in Chapter~4. A general-purpose retrieval model should perform effectively across the diverse semantic domain space rather than optimize for a single matching pattern.

\subsection{Video Embedding Models}
\label{sec:bc-embedder}

Modern video retrieval systems rely on embedding-based approaches, where queries and videos are mapped to dense vector representations in a shared semantic space, reducing retrieval to nearest neighbor search in the embedding space.

Let $E_q: \mathcal{Q} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$ and $E_v: \mathcal{V} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the query and video embedding functions, which map inputs to $d$-dimensional vectors. The relevance between a query $q$ and a video $v$ is computed as the cosine similarity between their embeddings:
\begin{equation}
    s(q, v) = \frac{E_q(q) \cdot E_v(v)}{\|E_q(q)\| \, \|E_v(v)\|}
\end{equation}

The embedding functions are implemented as neural network encoders. Depending on the underlying architecture, these encoders can be broadly categorized into two families: those built upon CLIP~\citep{radford2021learning}, which employ a dual-encoder architecture with modality-specific encoders for text and video respectively, and those based on Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), which adopt a unified architecture that processes text, images, and video through the same model. The architectural distinctions and representative models of each family are discussed in detail in \S\ref{sec:lr-embedding}. The challenge of training such models for heterogeneous retrieval conditions is addressed in Chapter~\ref{ch:universal-video-retrieval}.

\subsection{Spatiotemporal Sampling}
\label{sec:bc-spatiotemporal}

Before a video can be processed by an embedding model, it must be converted into a discrete sequence of visual inputs through spatiotemporal sampling. Since encoding every frame at full resolution is computationally prohibitive~\citep{hu2022mmsampler}, this preprocessing step critically determines both the available visual evidence and the retrieval quality~\citep{guo2025gve}.

Temporal sampling selects $F$ frames from a video of duration $T$. The most common strategy is uniform sampling, where frames are evenly spaced across the video duration.

Spatial sampling determines the visual resolution or token representation per frame. For ViT-based architectures, this involves converting frames into sequences of visual tokens, where the token count $S$ per frame determines the spatial detail preserved.

The combination of temporal frame count $F$ and spatial token count $S$ per frame defines the total input size $F \times S$ for the embedding model. This decomposition into orthogonal spatiotemporal dimensions allows systems to trade off temporal coverage against spatial detail under a fixed computational budget~\citep{zhu2025dynamic}.

The sampling configuration used during model training, denoted $(F_{\text{train}}, S_{\text{train}})$, may differ from that used during inference, denoted $(F_{\text{test}}, S_{\text{test}})$. In practice, inference often operates under stricter computational constraints, leading to a density mismatch between training and deployment. The impact of this mismatch on retrieval quality, and the extent to which retrieval performance depends on query granularity, are central questions in the \textbf{observational scale} of video retrieval, the fidelity and selectivity with which visual evidence is preserved for embedding generation.

\subsection{Large-Scale Search: ANNS and Retrieval Systems}
\label{sec:bc-search}

When a video retrieval system is deployed at scale, the candidate corpus may contain millions to billions of videos. Exhaustive similarity computation between a query embedding and every candidate becomes computationally prohibitive, motivating the development of \textbf{Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search (ANNS)} algorithms that achieve sub-linear query time by building index structures for selective exploration of the database.

Three main categories of index structures have been developed for ANNS. Tree-based methods recursively partition the vector space using hierarchical structures but suffer from the ``curse of dimensionality'' as dimensionality increases. Hashing-based methods map high-dimensional vectors to compact binary codes using locality-sensitive hashing (LSH)~\citep{indyk1998approximate}, enabling fast similarity estimation through Hamming distance. Graph-based methods construct a proximity graph where nodes represent database items and edges connect similar items. During retrieval, a greedy traversal starting from entry points iteratively expands the candidate set by examining neighbors of visited nodes. The Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) algorithm~\citep{malkov2018efficient} builds a multi-layered graph where upper layers provide sparse long-range connections for coarse navigation and lower layers provide dense local connections for fine-grained search. Graph-based methods have demonstrated superior performance on high-dimensional data and are widely adopted in production systems~\citep{wang2021comprehensive}.

A key property for efficient graph traversal is the \textbf{triangle inequality}: for any three points $a$, $b$, $c$ in a metric space, $d(a, c) \leq d(a, b) + d(b, c)$. When this property holds, greedy graph traversal is guaranteed to follow optimal paths. However, learned neural relevance metrics often violate the triangle inequality~\citep{chen2022approximate}, forcing the exploration of redundant edges and degrading retrieval efficiency.

Modern large-scale video retrieval and recommendation systems adopt a retrieval-and-ranking architecture~\citep{cheng2016wide,covington2016deep}. The retrieval stage reduces a billion-scale candidate corpus to a thousand-scale candidate set using lightweight models, which then serves as input for more sophisticated ranking models. The two-tower architecture~\citep{yang2020mixed} is widely adopted for the retrieval stage: it encodes queries and items through separate neural networks into embeddings compared via efficient similarity metrics such as inner product or cosine similarity. This architecture enables pre-computation and indexing of item representations, allowing real-time retrieval through ANNS. Beyond simple distance metrics, recent works use DNN-based learnable metrics~\citep{dehghani2017neural,tay2018latent} to estimate relevance through high-dimensional computation over embeddings, capturing more complex interaction patterns than hand-designed similarity functions~\citep{tan2020fast,chen2022approximate}.

The ANNS infrastructure and retrieval-and-ranking paradigm described above are shared across video retrieval and large-scale recommendation systems: in both settings, embeddings are pre-computed and indexed, and queries are answered through graph-based approximate search. In video recommendation, the retrieval stage instantiates the same infrastructure problem faced by deployment-scale video retrieval: learned relevance signals must be searched efficiently over massive candidate collections. The \textbf{search scale} of video retrieval therefore concerns whether learned retrieval signals remain searchable and executable within such large-scale serving infrastructure. The challenges of designing search-compatible relevance metrics, efficient graph traversal, and GPU-accelerated execution under high concurrency are common to both domains.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:rw-literature}

This section surveys existing work along three research themes that span the contributions of this thesis: the evolution of video embedding models and their training paradigms (\S\ref{sec:lr-embedding}), the development of video retrieval benchmarks and evaluation protocols (\S\ref{sec:lr-benchmarks}), and the integration of learned retrieval metrics with large-scale graph-based search systems (\S\ref{sec:lr-search}). These three themes correspond to the semantic, observational, and search scaling challenges introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}, and together trace the end-to-end pipeline from representation learning through evaluation to deployment-scale retrieval.

\subsection{Video Embedding Models}
\label{sec:lr-embedding}

The development of video embedding models can be traced through two major phases. The first phase extended CLIP~\citep{radford2021learning} to video by introducing temporal modeling: CLIP4Clip~\citep{luo2022clip4clip} pioneered temporal aggregation over frame-level features, followed by InternVideo2~\citep{wang2024internvideo2}, X-CLIP~\citep{gorti2022x}, VideoCLIP-XL~\citep{wang2024videoclip}, and LanguageBind~\citep{zhu2024languagebind}, which developed more sophisticated spatiotemporal encoders. These models, all built on the dual-encoder paradigm, achieved strong results on standard text-to-video benchmarks but were constrained by their modality-specific architecture and limited handling of complex language and long visual contexts~\citep{wang2025internvideo2,li2025improving}. The text encoder cannot process visual inputs, and the video encoder cannot process textual queries, limiting their applicability to composed or visual retrieval tasks.

The second phase shifted to MLLM-based backbones that process multiple modalities within a unified architecture. Models such as LLaVE~\citep{lan2025llave}, UNITE~\citep{kong2025modality}, and VLM2Vec-V2~\citep{meng2025vlm2vec} demonstrated that leveraging the language understanding and visual generalization of large pretrained models could yield strong embedding performance. Subsequent models including GME~\citep{Zhang_2025_CVPR}, BGE-VL~\citep{zhou2024megapairs}, UniME~\citep{gu2025breaking}, and B3~\citep{thirukovalluru2025breaking} further advanced the MLLM-based paradigm. Qwen3-VL-Embedding~\citep{qwen3vlembedding} showed the potential of large-scale supervised training for MLLM-based embeddings. Despite these advances, a systematic evaluation of such models across diverse retrieval tasks and semantic domains has been lacking, leaving their true generalization capabilities unclear.

A further challenge lies in the heterogeneity of multi-task supervision. When training data spans diverse query formats (textual, composed, visual) and semantic domains (coarse-grained, fine-grained, partially relevant, long-context, long-video), naively mixing them can lead to gradient interference: gradient signals from complex tasks may be submerged by simpler ones, leading to suboptimal convergence. Curriculum learning~\citep{bengio2009curriculum}, which schedules training examples from easier to harder tasks, offers a principled approach to such optimization challenges, but its application to heterogeneous multi-task training for video retrieval embeddings has not been systematically explored. This gap motivates the curriculum-based training methodology proposed in Chapter~\ref{ch:universal-video-retrieval}.

Existing scaling studies have primarily examined model size and training data volume. Recent work further reports power-law behavior with respect to frame count and caption density on short clips~\citep{zhu2025dynamic}. However, the interaction among spatial density, temporal density, long-form video, and query granularity remains insufficiently understood. This gap motivates the controlled observational-scaling study in Chapter~\ref{ch:spatiotemporal-scaling}.

\subsection{Video Retrieval Benchmarks and Evaluation}
\label{sec:lr-benchmarks}

Text-to-video retrieval has progressed from matching coarse phrases to parsing fine-grained spatio-temporal descriptions. Early datasets established the foundational paradigm: MSR-VTT~\citep{xu2016msr} introduced large-scale video-caption pairing with short clips, while DiDeMo~\citep{anne2017localizing} focused on temporal moment localization.

Subsequent benchmarks extended the scope in complementary directions. In the fine-grained dimension, CaReBench~\citep{xu2025carebench} incorporates spatio-temporal grounding annotations, UltraVideo~\citep{ultravideo} targets scene understanding, and CameraBench~\citep{lin2025towards} evaluates camera motion comprehension. In the long-video dimension, LoVR~\citep{cai2025lovr} targets retrieval over hour-long videos, while TVR~\citep{lei2020tvr} and ActivityNet Captions~\citep{krishna2017dense} provide fine-grained temporal annotations for partially relevant video retrieval~\citep{dong2022partially}. Beyond text-to-video retrieval, new paradigms have emerged, including composed queries with text-image pairs (e.g., CoVR~\citep{ventura2024covr,hummel2024egocvr}, MomentSeeker~\citep{yuan2025momentseeker}) and purely visual queries in egocentric contexts~\citep{liu2021activity}. Additional benchmarks such as VDC~\citep{chai2024auroracap} provide multi-granular caption annotations, and PE-Video~\citep{bolya2025perception} and DREAM-1K~\citep{wang2024tarsier} offer keyword-based and event-based retrieval annotations, respectively.

Despite this progress, evaluation remains fragmented. Each benchmark addresses only one or two dimensions of the retrieval problem, covering either query modality or semantic granularity but rarely both. Models are optimized and tested on isolated benchmarks, preventing a holistic understanding of their generalization across heterogeneous retrieval settings. No existing evaluation setting provides the combination of long-form visual evidence and multi-granular queries required to study how retrieval performance scales with query specificity across diverse semantic conditions.

These gaps motivate two complementary efforts in this thesis. Chapter~\ref{ch:universal-video-retrieval} introduces a unified evaluation protocol across 16 existing datasets, establishing a standardized diagnostic framework that exposes systemic capability deficiencies across current models. Chapter~\ref{ch:spatiotemporal-scaling} introduces MG-LVR, the first benchmark that combines long-form videos with multi-granular query categorization, enabling the first controlled study of how spatiotemporal sampling interacts with retrieval granularity. The scaling analysis built upon MG-LVR further reveals a training-inference density mismatch that motivates VEterfall, a progressive dense-to-sparse self-distillation framework~\citep{hinton2015distilling} for efficient video embedding inference.

\subsection{Large-Scale Retrieval and Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search}
\label{sec:lr-search}

As defined in \S\ref{sec:bc-search}, graph-based ANNS methods such as HNSW~\citep{malkov2018efficient} have become the dominant approach for high-dimensional similarity search, with subsequent works developing K-nearest neighbor graphs~\citep{fu2016efanna}, relative neighborhood graphs~\citep{jayaram2019diskann}, and GPU-optimized constructions~\citep{ootomo2024cagra}. These methods achieve sub-linear query time by constructing proximity graphs that enable greedy traversal toward the query, but their effectiveness depends critically on the properties of the similarity metric used during traversal. In parallel, the retrieval metrics used in practice have evolved from simple distance functions such as cosine similarity to learned DNN-based metrics that capture more complex relevance patterns (as discussed in \S\ref{sec:bc-search}).

This evolution parallels the development of DNN-based recommender systems~\citep{zhang2019deep}. Neural collaborative filtering uses nonlinear interaction functions to improve preference modeling~\citep{he2017neural}, while two-tower architectures support efficient retrieval by separately encoding users and items~\citep{yang2020mixed}. User-based and item-based collaborative filtering provide complementary ways to organize these relevance signals~\citep{ko2022survey,zhang2020alleviating,xue2019deep}. At deployment scale, the central challenge is to retain the richer relationships captured by learned metrics while preserving compatibility with efficient indexed search.

A critical challenge arises at the intersection of these two trends: neural relevance metrics often violate the triangle inequality~\citep{chen2022approximate}, a property that ensures optimal pathfinding in graph traversal (see \S\ref{sec:bc-search}). This violation forces the exploration of redundant edges and degrades retrieval efficiency, as the greedy traversal may follow paths that appear promising according to the learned metric but do not lead to the true nearest neighbors. Early industrial frameworks such as SL2G~\citep{tan2020fast} first used DNN-based metrics for graph-based ANNS in real-world recommendations, demonstrating that learned metrics could improve retrieval accuracy despite their incompatibility with graph traversal. NANN~\citep{chen2022approximate} extended this by improving the DNN-based metric for better accuracy and adaptivity, but the fundamental tension between metric expressiveness and search compatibility remained unresolved.

Beyond metric design, the computational efficiency of large-scale retrieval under high concurrency poses additional challenges. In production systems serving hundreds of millions of users, retrieval requests arrive asynchronously, each triggering divergent search paths through the graph. The resulting irregular neural computations, where each path visits a different set of candidate items requiring relevance evaluation, lead to frequent GPU kernel launches with fixed latency overhead that dominates runtime when individual computations are small~\citep{liu2022lion,liu2021jizhi}. The co-design of learned retrieval metrics with graph traversal efficiency and GPU-parallel execution remains an open problem. This gap motivates the search-scale contributions proposed in Chapter~\ref{ch:scale-efficient-retrieval}, which introduces GMP-GR, a framework coupling a Multi-relational User-Item Relevance Metric Learning~(MUIRML) objective with a Hierarchically Parallel Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search~(HiPANNS) algorithm to address the shared infrastructure challenges of large-scale embedding retrieval in production.
